## Proposal

I am a philosopher turned builder. I taught cultural analysis at the University of Amsterdam for over a decade before leaving academia in 2016 to build knowledge and learning infrastructure for organizations. Today I run Rizom, where I build and deploy AI agents inside organizations — which is how I found the problem this proposal addresses.

**The problem.** When organizations adopt AI agents, agency passes through the machine while accountability becomes difficult to assign. Agents retrieve, draft, route, and prioritize; they shape decisions without a named role, a provenance trail, or a path for an affected person to contest the result. I call this *responsibility laundering*. It is not a new gap — Helen Nissenbaum named the "problem of many hands" in 1996, and Madeleine Clare Elish showed how "moral crumple zones" let a token human absorb blame for systems they could not control — but agentic AI makes the gap routine, and current incentives select for it. Frictionless assistants sell; bounded, contestable systems don't demo as well. Meanwhile the deployment layer where this happens is thinly documented: MIT's NANDA project found that most enterprise GenAI pilots it studied produced no measurable P&L impact, and failures are quietly absorbed or rebranded rather than reported.

**The zero-to-one idea.** Nobody has published a design standard for accountable AI agents that an engineer can implement and a procurement officer can require. I am building one, with receipts. My open-source infrastructure ([github.com/rizom-ai/brains](https://github.com/rizom-ai/brains)) already runs the pattern in real organizations. I call it *situated agents*: a named role, bounded scope, provenance-carrying output, explicit escalation, and a genuine contestation path.

**Why this scales.** Standards are leverage. A single published, implementable standard changes procurement conversations everywhere at once: the moment buyers can ask "does this agent meet the situated-agent spec?", the default flips from frictionless to accountable — without waiting for regulation. Open-sourcing the reference implementation means the standard ships with proof it can be built.

**Why me.** I am not a frontier-lab insider. I am an insider of the layer where frontier claims are implemented, monetized, and encountered by the public: the organizations, procurement processes, and design decisions through which AI becomes consequential. I can move between philosophy, code, and institutional practice without treating them as separate worlds.

**What the grant buys.** Six months of focused work: (1) publish v1 of the situated-agent design standard; (2) harden the reference implementation for third-party adoption; (3) document real deployments as open-notebook research in a public, provenance-tracked knowledge base ([yeehaa.io/dashboard](https://yeehaa.io/dashboard)) so every claim can be traced to its source; (4) essays making the case publicly — "Your New AI Coworker Has No Job Description," "Nobody Reports a Failed AI Project."
