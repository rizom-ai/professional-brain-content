## Proposal

I am a philosopher turned builder. I taught cultural analysis at the University of Amsterdam for over a decade before leaving academia in 2016 to build knowledge and learning infrastructure for organizations. Today I run Rizom, where I build and deploy AI agents inside organizations. That work is how I found the problem this proposal addresses.

A consensus view I absolutely agree with: AI agents will transform how organizations work, and the productivity gains are real. I am not a skeptic of the technology. I deploy it for a living. My disagreement with the mainstream is narrower: the accountability design of the deployment layer is lagging far behind the capability curve, and that gap is a design problem, not a law of nature.

The problem. When organizations adopt AI agents, agency passes through the machine while accountability becomes difficult to assign. Agents retrieve, draft, route, and prioritize; they shape decisions without a named role, a provenance trail, or a path for an affected person to contest the result. I call this responsibility laundering. The gap itself is not new. Helen Nissenbaum named the "problem of many hands" in 1996, and Madeleine Clare Elish showed how a "moral crumple zone" lets a token human absorb blame for a system they could not control. What agentic AI adds is that it makes the gap routine, and current incentives select for it. Frictionless assistants sell; bounded, contestable systems don't demo as well. Meanwhile the deployment layer where this happens is thinly documented: MIT's NANDA project found that most enterprise GenAI pilots it studied produced no measurable P&L impact, and failures are quietly absorbed or rebranded rather than reported.

The zero-to-one idea. Nobody has published a design standard for accountable AI agents that an engineer can implement and a procurement officer can require. I am building one, with receipts. My open-source infrastructure (github.com/rizom-ai/brains) already runs the pattern in real organizations. I call it situated agents: a named role, bounded scope, provenance-carrying output, explicit escalation, and a genuine contestation path.

Why this scales. Standards are leverage. A single published, implementable standard changes procurement conversations everywhere at once: the moment buyers can ask "does this agent meet the situated-agent spec?", the default flips from frictionless to accountable, without waiting for regulation. Open-sourcing the reference implementation means the standard ships with proof it can be built.

Why me. I am not a frontier-lab insider. I am an insider of the layer where frontier claims are implemented, monetized, and encountered by the public: the organizations, procurement processes, and design decisions through which AI becomes consequential. I can move between philosophy, code, and institutional practice without treating them as separate worlds.

How long I've been at this: I have built agent infrastructure since 2019, when my Bots With an Attitude project at Waag's Planet B initiative planted the conceptual seed. The current codebase and the situated-agent pattern have been in development and live deployment at Rizom since early 2024.

What the grant buys. Six months of focused work: (1) publish v1 of the situated-agent design standard; (2) harden the reference implementation for third-party adoption; (3) document real deployments as open-notebook research in a public, provenance-tracked knowledge base (yeehaa.io/dashboard) so every claim can be traced to its source; (4) essays making the case publicly, with working titles like "Your New AI Coworker Has No Job Description" and "Nobody Reports a Failed AI Project."
